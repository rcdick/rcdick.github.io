Finishing my last Prelim on a Thursday in June, I boarded a plane that afternoon that took me from Europe through Vancouver to Whitehorse, Alaska. From there, I spent two weeks preparing for a 110 km traverse of Alaska's southeastern glaciers with the Juneau Icefield Research Program. The eight weeks on the icefield were the most profound experience of my life. Crossing on foot the north-south transect of the incomprehensibly large expanse of snow, firn, and ice that makes up the second-largest temperate icefield in the world, was indescribable. I threw myself into crevasses, dropping ten metres on a climbing rope tied to my harness with a knot I'd first learnt four weeks earlier; operated radar equipment worth hundreds of thousands of pounds like I had been a technician my entire life; dug shelters into kilometre-deep ice; and scaled peaks with newfound friends at three in the morning. The programme is what made the realisations below possible, and I'm genuinely grateful for that. If anything, it confirmed something I already suspected: that fieldwork is one of the most valuable parts of doing science, and that embedding yourself in the environment you study can **never** be replaced by observing it from a distance.

A week after coming home from two months in Alaska, I started an internship in the Bundestag – the German Parliament. It's the distance between those two worlds, not either one on its own, that this essay is actually about. Before I get to that, though, I want to take you to the ice. 

--

The Taku Glacier is the deepest and thickest alpine temperate glacier in the world. Having risen early at Field Camp 10 near Vantage Peak, we set out for my first scientific endeavour on the King of the Icefield. I rode on the back of a snowmachine while the rest of our research group skied the ten or so miles to our field site. We were there to dig a mass-balance pit – a five, sometimes seven metre deep hole in the snow. By measuring, layer by layer, the density of the compacted snow, these pits let us determine how much had accumulated over winter and how much ice the glacier had lost since. JIRP has the second-longest record of these measurements in the world, and by extension the mass-balance programme is one of the most valuable pieces of icefield data in the world for researchers interested in long-term climate trends.

As the snowmachine slowly came to a halt in the centre of the glacier, a field staffer jumped off and stepped onto the mile-deep ice. Now, you have to imagine what this place is like. Hundreds of square kilometres of ice make up the Juneau Icefield. In between the individual glaciers, mountains and rocky outcrops – what we call nunataks – dot the landscape. The Taku, though, spans close to ten kilometres at its widest point, far enough that even those mountains become small peaks in the distance. With the wind still, like it was that day, and the heat of the sun reflecting off the ice in your face, the first thing that hits you is the emptiness. There is no stimulus in the visual. A pristinely white basin stretches out in every direction. With each step, you sink slightly into a pillowy sea that you cannot see an end to. You think you can see the curvature of the world, at barely two thousand metres above sea level. It is overwhelmingly beautiful. Completely alien.

I remember vividly, and cannot really put into words, the realisation that in this moment, I was as physically alone as I had never been before, and likely would never be again. The two of us had taken the machine out to prepare the field site, which meant that, save for five friends maybe eight miles behind us, we were alone within the largest temperate expanse of snow and ice in the world.

--

Camp 18 was built in the 1960s in a striking location. Six shacks sitting on a ridgeline, overlooking the Vaughan Lewis Icefall and the confluence of the three glaciers in the valley immediately below camp. From there, myself and a group of seven others – including an IFMGA mountain guide who trusted us with more than I would have – set out for Camp 25, a shack clobbered together in the 80s that hadn't been visited in five years. We skied down the steep snowfield an hour's traverse to the north of camp, and crossed the blue ice below, watching out for moulins and crevasses. We tried scaling the icefall beside Boundary Peak 96, on skis, and switched back to crampons when our skins consistently failed at the 45° slope. The crossing of the glacier above the icefall marked our crossing back into Canada. And several easy kilometres of skiing later, we found ourselves at Mount Nesselrode, at C25 – a good thirty kilometres from the nearest camp. Eight of us, hot ramen, and instant mash. I firmly believe that no amount of data or reporting can replicate the lengths people go to in pursuit of scientific objectives – and that the impressions built along the way carry a kind of wisdom no report can either. I also think there is no way to convey the reality of the icefield in any shared medium beyond the shared experience itself.

An example. Camp 25 was built, maybe forty years ago, a metre or two above the glacier surface. Now, it sits thirty metres above the current snowline. Sure, take pictures, as I did, and show your folks back home. They'll say, "Oh, how devastating. So much ice gone. The world must've really been warming!" Post it on Instagram, and someone scrolling by will like, and comment, maybe even share the picture with their friends, and lament. 
But set a human in front of the face of rock, and give them the knowledge that their bunk, and the only promise of a night without frostbite, means scaling those thirty vertical metres. Make him look down from where he stood on the glacier surface, before he scrambled over jagged, loose scree, before his boots looked this beat up. And then have him look out onto the never-ending sea of ice stretching out beyond the immediate glacier. Make him feel that those ten minutes of struggle, those thirty metres he just scaled, are lost. The number of nunataks on the icefield must number in the hundreds, and those thirty metres are lost at each of those nunataks.

I think the isolation, and the physical reality of my life at C25, made me realise, more clearly than any figure in any report ever could, what four decades of change actually look like. The gap – between a shack once sunk into the ice and a shack now standing thirty metres above it – is data about how our planet has changed. It is also, to almost everyone who will ever decide what to do with our planet, and our societies and communities, completely illegible. 

-- 

That science-policy disconnect is my argument. Glaciology, and the geoscience subdisciplines in general, increasingly sit upstream of public policy. Decisions that matter to much more than just individual communities are directly influenced by the output of the scientific community. Just take our critical minerals supplies, disaster resilience planning, infrastructure decisions, and isolate them from geoscience data streams. Then try to mine more copper in the next twenty years than since the beginning of human history, or try and protect the population of your nation from floods and hurricanes and, god forbid, sea level rise. The cryosphere community contributes a disproportionately significant deal of knowledge to these decisions. Sea level projections, glacial lake outburst flood prediction, water security reports for downstream communities. To throw a couple of numbers at the reader, 1.9 billion people – 22% of the world's population – live downstream of snowpack and glaciers and depend on them as their main source of drinking water [[source]](https://theconversation.com/the-worlds-mountain-water-towers-are-melting-putting-1-9-billion-people-at-risk-128501). Let's read that again. A quarter of the world's population literally drinks the melt water coming off of glaciers. Even under the most optimistic scenarios, with global warming limited to 'only' 1.5°C, half of all glaciers could disappear entirely by 2100 [[source]](https://www.carbonbrief.org/half-of-worlds-glaciers-to-disappear-with-1-5c-of-global-warming). A quarter of the world's population limited to half of its drinking water needs? That cannot and will not be sustainable. The knock-on effects for the world are clear; even leaving economics out of the picture the overwhelming migration patterns and societal devastation are much too understated, in my opinion. If the reader is more into economics, as I am myself: $4 trillion of global GDP is at risk of being wiped out from the loss of glacier-fed freshwater. This hits agriculture, infrastructure, real estate, power generation, and tourism hardest, with regional productivity losses of up to 6% in some areas [[source]](https://www.unepfi.org/themes/ecosystems/world-water-day-2025-why-glacier-preservation-matters-for-finance/). This is already happening right now. Hydropower generation dropped to a 20-year low in the US in 2023 – not because of an Administration's policy priorities – but because of low snowpack in the West [[source]](https://www.kuow.org/stories/u-s-hydropower-output-drops-to-20-year-low-as-northwest-snowpack-shrinks). WA and OR hydropower generation fell 10% below the previous 20-year low [[source]](https://www.kuow.org/stories/u-s-hydropower-output-drops-to-20-year-low-as-northwest-snowpack-shrinks). Hydropower supplies easily more than half of the electricity in those states, whose electricity companies lost revenue of close to $200 million in that year alone. 

I've now told you what I've learnt through geosciences fieldwork and why I think the geosciences are important. But you've heard all this before. The scientists and politicians also know. Every up-and-coming mayor and senior senator knows of the climate problem, and the political 'green' parties of the world have not failed to make it their utmost priority to tell everyone the same thing over and over again. The geoscience discipline still treats communicating that science to the people who act on it as a soft skill, something you pick up if you're lucky, something that should happen naturally if you only operate your radar well enough. But the bottleneck to getting futureproof, science-driven policy isn't data. It's a translation problem. The populist, extreme-right parties who have been sweeping through states in the formerly staunchly democratic West capitalize on every single climate policy reform. Peoples' expenses are easily the most politicized issue. Any single piece of legislation that does not add dollars back to citizens' individual wallets is a percentage point less for a democratic party in the next election. The feeling of financial freedom is constrained by common-sense, climate relevant policies. Conversely, and paradoxically, the policies we must adopt to rescue humanity from the looming abyss appear to the people that are closest to that abyss as a burden. Therefore – and I say this heavy-heartedly, as expert governments are easy traps to get caught in – I used to think the answer was to get more scientists into office. But my own subtitle argues against me: the moment a scientist becomes a politician, something is lost, not gained – they stop being the thing that made them useful in the first place. The gap doesn't need more people crossing it and disappearing on the other side. It needs someone standing in the middle, permanently, whose job is the crossing itself. 

What I actually think we're missing isn't scientist-politicians. It's translation treated as a discipline – trained, funded, and staffed, the way we train and fund the radar surveys and mass-balance pits themselves. JIRP is unusual in taking the translation problem seriously. Science communication sits explicitly in the curriculum alongside glaciology, climate processes, and geomorphology. That structure alone is an implicit argument: that a scientist who can write papers and publish in Nature but can't explain why it matters to the average Joe has only done half the job. Not a nice-to-have module bolted onto the real science. A third leg, alongside data collection and analysis, of what doing science responsibly requires. 

None of this is an argument for dumbing science down. There's a huge risk on the other side of that thought: a finding simplified past the point of accuracy is worse than one that never reaches a policymaker at all, because it gets acted on with false confidence. I think the question of global warming is a case study in that effect. "The world is going to end with what our cars and planes and industry are doing to the atmosphere" is a simplified translation of a finding from twenty years ago that, while true, was not accurate twenty years ago. It alienated half of the world's population in the developing so-called global south, alienated half of the West's population that relied on manufacturing as their source of income, alienated anyone with a car as electric vehicles were pushed into the masses. Of course we are getting ever closer to the point at which we have to confront the fact that our world will fundamentally change as it warms. But I think that consolidated efforts in specific directions, 'spikes' as founders like to call them, would have been much more effective than blanket emissions cutting. A consolidated effort around a limited number of spikes allows for political momentum to persist, momentum that consistent blanket climate policy and droning on about the dangers of everything destroys so well. The Montreal Protocol, which phased out CFCs and HCFCs is my favorite example in that regard. Originally targeted for depleting the ozone layer, they're also extremely potent greenhouse gases, so the ban ended up being one of the most effective climate actions ever taken. 

To return to the point of not dumbing results down: The scientists I found most convincing on the icefield weren't the ones who tried to translate simply – they were the ones who knew exactly which details could be lost without losing the truth of the result. They are the people who know that the relevance of decisions that might be taken based on data depends on the relevance of the data itself. It's a really hard skill to be good at, as it requires that you know, have immersed yourself, lived, and built experience in both circles, the determinant layer and the application layer. That's a discipline in itself, and it's the one I think glaciology, and Earth science more broadly, under-trains for. 

The stakes of getting our next ten years of Earth-related policies right are massive and only growing. Glacial retreat is already reshaping everything from water availability to mineral access in ways that touch policy far outside of the jurisdiction of modern geoscience departments. The same fieldwork that produces a mass-balance number also, several translation steps downstream, shapes decisions about reservoir management, mining permits, and water rights. The scientists doing that fieldwork, the scientists that know what the data really means, the scientists that are the only chance at knowing what is relevant today, are usually not in the room when those decisions get made. The ones who can translate well are more likely to be.
