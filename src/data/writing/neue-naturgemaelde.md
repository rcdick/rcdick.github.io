> **TL;DR** Ryan Orbuch argues AI's next frontier is a "nature model", a foundation model of the planet trained on direct observations. I think he's largely right about what to build and wrong about why. Life isn't Gaia's guardian; it's an infighting guest, so wiring AI into the biosphere won't make it a steward. What the engine needs is three things Orbuch's essay doesn't answer: which data to trust, which effects are ours, and who gets to ask it questions. Natural hazards (defensible events) need early warning; human-made ones (offensible events) need attribution, from a record we can never rerun. The binding constraint on data isn't cost but relevance. Most of the technical pieces already exist; nobody has put them into one physics-bounded system scored against real observations. The first product should be a marginal-dollar engine that tells you which measurement to buy next. And legibility isn't action: whoever poses the questions holds the power.

Imagine yourself on a glacier, on an endlessly white expanse of snow and ice. Before you is a pit in the snow, five or seven metres deep. After working hard for a day to get this far, you measure layer after layer the density of the snow in the column below to work out how much mass the glacier gained over winter and how much has melted away since. Say this is the Taku Glacier, on the Juneau Icefield in southern Alaska, where I spent this summer. We dig perhaps six, maybe seven mass-balance pits in a season on this glacier. They are some of the most valuable measurements in glaciology, and each of them is a single Excel table of a few values for a single point on the ice.

Now, this glacier is almost ten kilometres across at its widest, meaning that a lot of what we say about the glacier as a whole is an inference from a handful of pits, a great deal of physics, and judgement about which holes to trust (Aside: that judgement sometimes comes from singular people. On one pit-digging trip, we were so unsure if the data we collected made sense that we satellite-phoned the one USGS glaciologist who was familiar data series trends on the Taku.) Fieldwork can't be replaced by distant observation.

And those pieces of information, together with a good ten terabytes of Landsat imagery, Juneau airport weather records, some PhD student's one-year lapse rate profile, two NOAA pollution measurement flights, and fifteen disagreeing reanalysis models, should tell us how the Alaskan winter season played out this year. The point I'm trying to make is that Earth data are sparse, hard to get, and that they present an integration problem, because they don't all measure the same direct processes, but are all still related intimately to each other.

[Ryan Orbuch recently argued](https://naturalgeneralintelligence.ai/) that AI's next frontier is a "nature model": a foundation model of the planet's state and dynamics, trained on direct observations of the Earth rather than on human text or climate reanalysis. I think he is largely right about what should be built. I disagree about why, about parts of how, and about where the boundary should sit.

## Life is an infighting guest

Orbuch opens with Lovelock's Gaia Hypothesis, that life actively maintains the conditions that keep the planet habitable. I think there is much evidence in the Earth Sciences for planetary homeostasis; the silicate weathering feedbacks that keep carbon sequestered, constant slow streams of carbon dioxide leaking out of the crust that temporally bound whole-world glaciations. The biosphere also shapes the Earth, through oxygen generation, soils, carbon drawdown, but shaping is not the same as maintaining.

However, I don't think – and this is the exact pushback of Peter Ward's Medea Hypothesis, and of Doolittle and Dawkins before him – that life has any right to assert itself as a proponent of that self-regulation if, on geological timescales, those actually relevant to the planet, it does anything but keep the world spinning. From the first photosynthesizing organisms, which plunged the Earth into winter (the Huronian, Sturtian, and Marinoan 'snowball' Earths were all at least partly linked to new life) and wiped out much of the life of its day, life is an infighting guest disrespectful to its host. The long-term carbon 'thermostat' of weathering is largely geochemical. And there is an observer-selection effect too – we find ourselves on a planet where the feedbacks hold, because nobody is around to interrupt me from a planet where they didn't. Worlds that life had reliably kept habitable would not have been built on an ivory tower of so much luck. The only relevant distinction into the late Holocene is the rate with which homo sapiens et al are perturbing the natural world. As I continue, maybe we'll find that that's a unique opportunity too.

This matters for the natural prediction we build. If life is a participant rather than a guardian, Orbuch's reason for wiring AI into the biosphere falls apart. He argues that "our values arise in part from the requirements of the human organism", and that AI, "the first intelligence on earth which doesn't breathe", therefore needs to be deliberately connected to the observing system instead. But breathing never made life a steward of the planet, and it won't make an AI one either. I think we should want to avoid that wiring. An intelligence tapped into the planet will reduce the pain threshold, the point at which we say yes to ridiculous ideas, to influence the biosphere in ways nobody can predict, which is not the problem we are trying to solve. Orbuch's loop does keep humans in it, and I'm not claiming otherwise. I just don't think humans could be effective stewards of a natural world that is much more complex than they are, nor that a natural prediction engine should get them to a point where they think they can. Or should. Instead, a natural prediction engine should show what perturbations to the Earth system are likely to cause what effects, allowing us to determine causality, and with that inform those who decide.

I want to make a couple of points. Orbuch has written a good specification for a planetary prediction engine, and he stops exactly where it gets difficult. He wants a model that makes the planet legible. A model that only does that is a liability. It has to know which data to trust, which effects are ours, and who gets to ask it questions, and his essay doesn't answer any of the three.

## 1. Causality is difficult. We need to distinguish between defensible and offensible events.

Natural risks to humanity appear to me to fall into two groups, and the distinction matters more than it first appears. Some are not of our making, and that intuitively makes them risks to defend against. Take supervolcanic eruptions, large impacts, and extreme space weather. The same year that Fitzroy's charts anticipated the Royal Charter storm, the Carrington storm knocked out telegraph networks regionwide. Events not of our making, defensible events, need to be explicitly forecasted by a prediction engine that works on natural data and is constrained by physics, as Orbuch argues. They are, for now, not preventable, which makes real, causality-based prediction vital for humanity. As we perturb the natural world in an effort to make defensible events preventable, we need to be explicitly aware of the measurable good we are doing for humanity, and if we care about the natural world, the causal effects of our actions in that regard.

Others are human-made: climate change, fisheries collapse, groundwater depletion, nutrient pollution. These are what I would term offensible events. They are attributable to us, and so in principle reversible. I'd argue, differently to Orbuch, that while the prediction engines that work with these attributable events must be physics-bounded, they have zero RL data to work with. Reinforcement learning needs an action you can repeat and a response you can watch again. The Earth gives us every perturbation exactly once, uncontrolled, with no reset button and no control planet to compare it against. And they are inherently reversible, but we don't know, not in the slightest, how, and no amount of passively collected data makes that possible. Data can tell us what happened after we acted. It cannot tell us what would have happened had we not.

That doesn't leave us empty-handed. It leaves us with evidence instead of experiments. What we need in those genuinely uncharted, untested cases is an engine that combines reanalysis and outputs from the first engine with catalogued, known, past human perturbations and their effects on the natural system. Piecing together the morsels of human-nature effect evidence we have requires a repository of natural feedback loop knowledge, but also requires inference. That is not RL data. It is a record to learn from, and the next section is about how.

Take Antarctic ice-shelf buttressing [[source]](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-024-01297-8). The shelves floating at the ice sheet's edge hold back the glaciers behind them, and as warm water thins them, those glaciers speed up. That loss is ours, and in principle it is reversible: cool the water, let the shelves thicken, and the buttress comes back. But the way back is far slower than the way down. In their models, even sustained cooling brings the buttress back without stopping the ice loss, and every shortcut proposed so far, like curtains on the seabed in front of Thwaites, is a new perturbation with consequences of its own. Reversible does not mean easy, and it does not mean clean.

The two groups ask different things of a prediction engine. For the first, you need early warning. For the second, you need attribution and a reliable estimate of what an intervention would actually do, which is a much harder problem to solve. A model that doesn't distinguish between the two will blame us for the weather and excuse us for the climate.

## 2. A natural prediction engine must be built about knowing what data to trust and what data to pursue.

Where I think Orbuch is right is the engine's specification. A nature model should be general-purpose and not built around one agent's goal. It should definitively know the planet's current state, estimate the states it does not know (including the parts nobody observes or thinks to observe), simulate futures, and decide which new observations would resolve the most important uncertainties. Pure lab automation can't do this, because it is limited by the data we can collect and by whether that data is relevant. For some types of data, volume is not the constraint. If weather centres already only ingest about a per mille of what the planet's sensors collect, the discriminant is knowing what to keep. Assimilation is already a theory of that, and the question is how far we can push it.

For other types of data, volume uniquely serves to open doors to natural systems we can only infer. Here, I part ways with him on sampling. He frames it through cost, since each sample is a physical measurement of the real world, and the Earth can't be queried, paused or rerun. I don't think cost is the binding constraint. Sensors, launches and connectivity are getting cheaper every year, as he shows himself. Relevance is. What we lack is knowledge of which observations to trust and which matter. Classical assimilation weights each instrument by its error statistics, which tells you how far to believe a measurement. It does not tell you whether the quantity it measures matters for what you are trying to predict. That is a real scientific problem, not just an engineering one.

For forecasting, predictive relevance is enough, and you can measure it: hold an observation stream out and see what the model loses. Weather centres have run exactly these data-denial experiments for decades. For intervention, it isn't. Why weight something if you don't know whether it causes the outcome? The only clean evidence we have comes from accidental experiments, such as eruptions, changes in shipping fuel rules and industrial regions cleaning up their air. A nature model needs forcings as explicit inputs for exactly this reason. It has to learn from the record how the system responded to what was done to it, not only what was seen next. That means measuring the input as well as the output. When the IMO cut the sulphur allowed in shipping fuel in 2020, we knew exactly when, where and by roughly how much the forcing changed, which is what makes the ship-track clouds that thinned out afterwards usable as evidence. Pinatubo's fifteen to twenty million tonnes of sulphur dioxide in 1991 did the same for volcanic cooling.

That also means knowing what new data streams to measure with each incremental dollar. A dollar spent on an instrument measuring a perturbance in an area the engine deems most relevant to prove the Earth feedback next-highest on the list of interconnection priorities is a dollar well spent. A dollar spent on a seismic array measuring the same signals as an Android phone might not be.

Orbuch also observes that the leading AI weather models mostly train on reanalysis rather than on observations. He finds it surprising. I think the reason is simple: reanalysis is complete, gridded and regular, while observations are sparse, irregular, biased and heterogeneous. The cost of that convenience is that the model learns from a blend of measurement and simulation, with no record of which is which, so it cannot learn what any observation is worth.

That doesn't mean reanalysis is worthless. If you pretrain on an archive, reanalysis gives a dense, cheap prior on how the natural system moves. The archive also holds the raw observations (AIFS-DOP was trained on forty years of them), and these should be provenance-labelled so the model can tell them from simulated content. Then you move into observation space. Fine-tune on the raw observations and score against held-out ones. This is what ECMWF's AIFS-DOP did, with 16 GPUs and four days of training. Then you replay history. Humanity's natural world observation system has been built up in steps: satellite sounders in the late 70s, GPS radio occultation in the mid-2000s. Each new instrument is a natural experiment in information value. You can go even more granular than that. Did the Argo floats improve model value, and by how much? Weather centres answer that question one stream at a time, rerunning forecasts with it withheld; a learned model can ask it across the whole history at once. A launched, real-time assimilation system, with the learned model as the background, self-corrects with well-labelled constantly ingested observations.

I'd also start cheap and end expensive, deliberately. AIFS-DOP is cheap relative to its skill. That is a feature of the early stage, not a limitation. A cheap model lets you test architecture and data choices quickly, find out which streams matter, and only then pay to scale. Each rung of the ladder is judged against held-out observations. Longitudinal records can't be recreated. JIRP's mass-balance series, the second-longest glacier record in the world, is exactly Orbuch's point that "nothing other than the real Earth can manufacture longitudinal observations." The Earth is the observing system.

## 3. A natural prediction engine must have core differences to other forms of GI.

Almost none of what follows is new, and that is the point. Observation tokens, Perceiver-style encoders, data-denial experiments, physics enforced in the architecture, hybrid models like NeuralGCM: all of it exists, mostly in separate labs solving separate problems. Nobody has put it into one system. That is what we should be arguing for.

**Tokenise observations, not cells.** Each measurement becomes a token, rather than each grid location: position, time, instrument, channel, value, uncertainty…. That unmeshing has several routes: setting encoders that cross-attend into a fixed latent array (as in Perceiver-style models), so cost scales with the latent's size and not with the number of observations, or by implementing neural fields that can be queried at any coordinate, or by graph meshes acting as a purely internal convenience, not as the interface to the data. Decoders need to be queryable at arbitrary points so the model can be scored against any data from any instrument, and that new data sources can be tested and verified against the current standard of modelling. Ask the model what a seismometer in southern Utah is going to record when a magnitude 8 earthquake hits off Japan, and you can score its answer ten minutes later. Turn the question around, and that seismic signature in Utah tells you a tsunami might be on its way across the Pacific. Kind of a convoluted example, but you get the point: you always want the model to tell you what an instrument is going to observe, because that is the only thing the future can check.

**Let the backbone be boring.** Orbuch suspects an ordinary backbone may win, and I agree, and I also have an opinion as to why. Transformers tend to absorb the advantage of bespoke architectures once there is enough data. What carries the inductive bias is the interface. You have instrument and channel embeddings, positional encodings suited to a sphere, native handling of missing data, and masked-observation pretraining, in which the model predicts the observations hidden. That last one is self-supervised and naturally scored by the planet, and gives you the value of each stream as a by-product.

On assimilation versus machine learning, they are better at different things. Variational and ensemble assimilation carry explicit uncertainty, use observation operators derived from physics, and handle a new instrument whose physics is understood. But they are expensive, they need a model to run, and their error statistics are hand-tuned. End-to-end learning is fast and can learn observation operators and instrument biases directly, but it is weaker on calibrated uncertainty, exact conservation and extreme events. The aim is to learn what assimilation does, keeping its bookkeeping of uncertainty, not to abandon it.

**Hard-code the physics, but learn the approximate.** Orbuch rightly argues that conservation of mass, energy, and momentum, should be indigenously enforced by the architecture, through projection layers, not nudged by a loss term that can leak. Parametrisations are different, as they are human-authored guesses, and as models receive more data in the form of observations, the model should take them over. Simulation is fine as long as it's labelled as synthetic data, for counterfactuals the record can't supply, as long as the core model is not scored against it. The finding that models degrade as events become more extreme, while physics-based engines hold up but don't perform as well for modelling current states, is the argument for keeping exact constraints we definitively know exact.

I think the important point is that physics constraints and hard-fought-for data must work with each other. Orbuch wants the model to learn couplings freely from data and a respect for hard physics, and admits that the free version can drift from reality. That is a tension that isn't fully resolved. While it might be true that an unconstrained learning model may at first choose the simplest explanation over a reasoned feedback we've been investigating for decades, what argument is there against a pathway of discovery and self-testing based on new data streams becoming available? That is the method natural prediction needs to follow for the future anyways, and if it self-corrects, what is there to say its guess isn't as valid as mine? The resolution is that unconstrained and bounded are not opposites. The model should be free in what it learns, and bounded absolutely in what it may break. It may find a link between bushfire smoke and Southern Ocean clouds that nobody has written down. It may not create energy or mass to get to that conclusion. Inside those walls, its guess is as valid as mine, and the observations decide.

**One system, not coupled models.** I really like Orbuch's fourth principle, and I would go even further. Coupled models exchange only what researchers specify (and think of!) in advance. Antarctic meltwater missing from the ocean module in Earth Sciences is the textbook case. Don't couple at all. Use one state, in which land, ocean, ice, atmosphere, and (perhaps the) biosphere are all represented, with their fluxes and reservoirs, so interactions are not limited to human-centric, relatively arbitrary interfaces. The side-effect is that this doesn't remove approximation. A single learned state is still an approximation. But, it is an approximation driven by data collection constraints, not by preconceived notions. It is an approximation learnt from data and tested against observations. It is an approximation that can also be wrong in ways nobody wrote down, which is why scoring model performance against verified and constructive observations matters so much.

**Cheap sensors, expensive prediction.** This leads on directly from the previous point. The more verifiable observations contribute to the feedbacks the model reproduces, the more a model can score itself. Eventually, AIFS-DOP-derived natural prediction engines are just going to have to grow beyond their leanness. The Earth is not a lean system. It is the highest possible dimension state of being, and we will only ever be able to approximate it. The more data about the natural world it ingests, the better its inference.

## 4. The marginal dollar should be the first product.

Orbuch ends his fifth principle by asking where the next marginal dollar of observation should go. Why not make that the first product. A marginal-dollar engine takes a budget and a decision, say a water authority deciding what to monitor, and ranks possible observations by expected information gain per dollar. I'd start there because:

- It is verifiable. You can check afterwards whether the measurement reduced uncertainty.
- It is read-only, so it asks nothing of the model that it shouldn't be doing.
- It serves buyers who already exist, in a system Orbuch rightly calls brittle and underfunded.
- It starts a flywheel. The observations it recommends are the ones that improve the model.

## 5. Legibility isn't action.

Orbuch assumes a better model makes consequences legible and stewardship follows. My point is that the data already exists and is still illegible to the people deciding. A planet-scale model could make the translation gap worse: more authoritative and more opaque. The "doesn't decide policy" clause is where he skips the hard part.

He admits the observing system is geopolitically entangled and that shared rivers need "one shared factual model." Indus or Nile riparian states don't fight over missing facts. They fight over allocation. A shared model doesn't dissolve that, and whoever runs it gains enormous power over what counts as the facts.

That leaves the question I find hardest. What do we do with a model that can tell us, with growing reliability, what the planet will do in response to us?

My answer is that it predicts and people, and honestly also companies, decide. It should be used to save the world, not to remake it. It is not for terraforming, and the choice of which futures to pursue stays a conscious human one. But I can't pretend that line is clean. Saving needs a baseline, and saving needs a knowledge of what saving means. Saved relative to what: the pre-industrial Earth, the Earth of fifty years ago, the Earth we have? What does saving mean: restoring a fisheries? Seeding clouds? Those are interventions. A model that can tell you the consequence of every dial invites you to turn every dial. And dials you think the model understands, but really is inferring, and getting wrong. Legibility itself is form of power. Whoever decides which questions the engine answers decides which futures look possible.

I don't have a rule for this. Why would I. What I do have is a suspicion that the answer is about governance, about who gets to pose the questions, more than about the technology. A pit tells you about one place on the glacier. The point of the engine is to tell us which pit to dig next, and no one is thinking about who gets to say what we do once we've dug it.
